# How many customer interviews are enough for market validation?

There is no universal number of customer interviews that proves market demand. The evidence is sufficient when the right people have been interviewed, important customer groups are represented, new interviews add little decision-relevant information and the findings are strong enough for the decision you need to make.

Test Before You Build’s standard €2,900 + VAT service includes 20 interviews. This is a defined scope for a focused project—not a universal rule or a limit. Broader questions, multiple segments or several markets may require a larger, separately scoped study.

## The short answer: there is no universal interview number

Five interviews may uncover important problems. Ten may reveal recurring patterns. Twenty may expose differences that were invisible in the first ten. None of these numbers automatically settles the commercial question.

The appropriate number depends on who you interview, how narrowly the target group is defined, how many distinct groups must be understood, what assumption you are testing, how much new information each interview adds and how difficult the decision would be to reverse.

### Interviews provide depth, not a statistical forecast

A qualitative interview sample is selected because participants can provide relevant information—not because every member of the wider market has an equal probability of being selected. Twenty interviews with the wrong people remain twenty irrelevant interviews.

Interviews can reveal whether people experience the proposed problem, how they deal with it today, what triggers them to act, who influences and pays for a solution, and what prevents change. Their purpose is to assess the strength, nature and conditions of demand—not to forecast market-wide revenue from a small qualitative sample.

### “Enough” depends on the decision

Five conversations may be enough to repair an early interview guide. A substantial development investment demands a higher standard: recurring patterns, relevant exceptions and an understanding of differences between the people involved in buying and using the solution.

> Do we have enough relevant evidence to make the next decision, and do we understand what remains uncertain?

## Five factors that determine how many interviews you need

### 1. How narrowly the target group is defined

A useful definition may specify industry, company size, geography, buying role, current solution and the event that creates a need to act. If the group is too broad, apparent contradictions may reflect different markets being treated as one.

### 2. How many distinct segments must be understood

A purchase may involve users, technical evaluators, managers, budget holders and procurement teams. If three groups must be understood, 15 interviews may be three small groups of five rather than one sample of 15.

### 3. How focused the commercial question is

“Will people buy this?” is too broad for a reliable interview answer. More useful questions concern the importance of the problem, the current alternative, the trigger for change, the buyer, approval criteria and barriers to adoption.

### 4. How much new information each interview adds

A new interview may reinforce a pattern, reveal an exception, explain a difference, contradict an assumption or add nothing that changes the interpretation. The test is whether it changes or strengthens the decision.

### 5. How consequential the decision is

A reversible experiment requires less evidence than a large, difficult-to-reverse investment. Consider development cost, time to market, opportunity cost, regulatory commitments and the cost of changing direction later.

## What research says about interview sample size

Qualitative research does not offer one universally correct sample size. Saturation describes a point at which further data collection produces little or no new information relevant to the analysis, but researchers have applied the term in different ways.

A systematic review by Hennink and Kaiser examined 23 empirical or model-based studies. In studies with relatively homogeneous populations and narrowly defined objectives, saturation was commonly reported within 9–17 interviews. More varied populations and deeper examination could require larger samples. This is not a universal market-validation rule. [Hennink and Kaiser, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34785096/).

Malterud, Siersma and Guassora’s information-power model proposes that fewer participants may be required when a sample contains highly relevant information for a focused study. It considers the study aim, sample specificity, established theory, interview quality and analysis strategy. [Malterud, Siersma and Guassora, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26613970/).

A systematic analysis found that sample-size justifications and uses of saturation vary considerably. This supports explaining how sufficiency was assessed rather than citing a number without context. [Vasileiou et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6249736/).

Academic findings can inform customer-research design. They do not turn a commercial interview project into a statistically representative forecast or guarantee future sales.

### What customer interviews can reveal about market demand

Customer interviews reveal whether realistic potential buyers or users experience the problem, how important it is, what they do about it today, why they might change and what could prevent them from buying or adopting a new solution.

When these signals recur across a carefully selected group, they provide meaningful evidence for deciding whether to BUILD, CHANGE or KILL the idea before committing to development. No validation method can guarantee future sales, but customer interviews can substantially reduce the risk of building around assumptions that the intended market does not share.

## A practical stopping rule

1. **Check participant relevance.** Are they realistic potential buyers, users or agreed members of the buying process?
2. **Review each segment separately.** Are the important groups sufficiently represented?
3. **Track new decision-relevant evidence.** Record themes, support, exceptions, contradictions and changes to the interpretation.
4. **Investigate contradictions.** They may reveal separate markets, different urgency or a missing purchasing condition.
5. **Match confidence to the decision.** Evidence sufficient for a prototype may not support a full development programme.
6. **State what remains uncertain.** A sound recommendation makes unresolved questions explicit.

> Stop because additional interviews are unlikely to change the current decision—not merely because a predetermined number has been reached.

## Warning signs that you do not yet have enough evidence

- Participants are mainly friends or existing supporters.
- They are interested in the subject but are not realistic buyers or users.
- Buyers and users have been combined despite different interests.
- An important segment is missing.
- The solution was presented before the current problem was understood.
- Questions encouraged praise or agreement.
- Answers concern hypothetical behaviour rather than actual experience.
- New interviews continue to reveal major decision-relevant themes.
- Contradictory evidence has been dismissed.
- Conclusions rely mainly on counting positive comments.

More interviews will not repair every problem. Correct weak participant selection or interview design before increasing the number.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is five customer interviews enough?

Five relevant interviews can reveal important problems and challenge early assumptions. Whether they are enough depends on the target group, the decision and what remains unresolved.

### Are ten customer interviews enough?

Ten carefully selected participants can provide strong initial evidence for a narrowly defined group. More may be needed when there are distinct segments, responses remain varied or the decision is difficult to reverse.

### Is the service limited to 20 interviews?

No. The standard €2,900 + VAT service includes 20 interviews. Projects involving multiple segments, buyer roles, countries, use cases or broader objectives can be scoped and priced separately.

### What does saturation mean in customer interviews?

It describes a point at which further interviews add little new information. Repetition does not guarantee future purchases, and a poorly selected sample can reach apparent repetition without representing the right people.

### What can customer interviews reveal about market demand?

They reveal whether potential buyers or users experience the problem, how important it is, what they do today, why they might change and what could prevent adoption. Recurring signals provide meaningful evidence for a BUILD, CHANGE or KILL decision.

### Should buyers and users be interviewed separately?

Yes, when their needs, incentives or decision authority differ.
