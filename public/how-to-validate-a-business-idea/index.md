# How to Validate a Business Idea Before You Build

**By [Dr. Kari Jääskeläinen](https://testbeforeyoubuild.com/about-kari-jaaskelainen/)**  
Published and updated 16 September 2026

Validate the assumptions that could make the idea succeed or fail before committing to development. Define the decision, identify the right customers, gather evidence about their problems and behaviour, and use explicit criteria to decide whether to BUILD, CHANGE or KILL.

## The short answer

A promising idea is not validated because people like it. It is validated when evidence supports the critical assumptions behind a real commercial decision: a meaningful problem, an identifiable customer, sufficient motivation to change, a workable buying process and a credible way to create and capture value.

## What business idea validation actually means

Business idea validation is a structured attempt to find out whether the assumptions behind an idea are strong enough to justify the next investment. It is not a search for compliments, a polished business plan or a single score that declares an idea valid.

The useful question is: **What must be true for this idea to work, and what evidence would change our decision?**

A business idea normally combines several claims: a particular group experiences an important problem, existing alternatives are inadequate, the proposed approach creates meaningful value, someone can and will make the buying decision, and the economics can work. These claims need different evidence.

No research method can guarantee future sales. Good validation makes the remaining uncertainty visible and reduces the chance of building around assumptions that the intended market does not share.

Market research can describe market size, competitors, trends and customer categories. Validation applies evidence to the specific assumptions and decision behind one idea. A large market does not establish that a particular customer will adopt a particular solution through a workable buying process.

## Start with the assumptions that must be true

- **Customer:** Can you identify realistic buyers, users and other decision-makers precisely enough to reach them?
- **Problem:** Does the target group experience the problem frequently or seriously enough to act?
- **Current behaviour:** What do customers do today, what does it cost them and why have they not already changed?
- **Value:** Would the proposed improvement matter compared with existing alternatives and doing nothing?
- **Buying process:** Who decides, who pays, what approvals are required and what could block adoption?
- **Commercial model:** Can the idea be delivered, sold and supported at economics that work for both sides?

Prioritise assumptions by consequence and uncertainty. Test first the assumption that would make the idea untenable if it proved false.

## A seven-step business idea validation process

1. **Define the decision.** State what you are deciding now: whether to invest, which segment to pursue, what problem to solve or which assumptions need another test.
2. **Write down the critical assumptions.** Separate claims about customers, problems, alternatives, buying behaviour, value, feasibility and economics.
3. **Define the target group.** Specify realistic buyers, users and decision-makers by role, situation, current behaviour and relevant trigger.
4. **Review existing evidence.** Examine alternatives, market structure, search behaviour, regulations, purchasing routes and previous research.
5. **Interview potential customers.** Investigate past and present behaviour, the consequences of the problem, current workarounds, buying criteria, decision authority and barriers to change.
6. **Test the most important remaining uncertainty.** Depending on the decision, use a prototype, landing page, paid pilot, pre-sale, procurement conversation or another experiment that produces stronger behavioural evidence.
7. **Compare the evidence with decision criteria.** Document support, contradictions, segment differences and unresolved questions. Decide whether to BUILD, CHANGE or KILL.

“Companies,” “consumers” or “healthcare professionals” are rarely precise enough target definitions. The people interviewed may be potential buyers, users or both. When users experience the problem but other people approve the budget, the groups should be represented and analysed separately.

Begin interviews with actual events: the last time the problem occurred, what the participant did, what it cost, who became involved and why the present solution was accepted. Presenting the proposed solution too early can turn discovery into a polite product review.

## Use the right evidence for the question

Different methods answer different questions:

- **Market and competitor research** examines market structure, alternatives, trends, constraints and possible segments.
- **Search and behavioural data** reveals existing interest, language, journeys and observable behaviour.
- **Customer interviews** examine problems, current behaviour, motivations, decision criteria and barriers.
- **Prototype or concept tests** examine understanding, usability, relevance and reactions to a proposed approach.
- **Pilots, pre-sales and procurement steps** test willingness to commit time, reputation, process or money.

Evidence becomes more useful when it is tied to a named assumption and a decision. A high search volume may show interest in a problem category. It does not by itself establish that your proposed product, customer segment, price and route to market fit together.

Customer interviews can reveal the strength, nature and conditions of market demand. When these signals recur across a carefully selected group, they provide meaningful evidence for deciding whether to continue, change direction or stop. Broader questions, multiple segments or several markets may require a larger interview project rather than treating one fixed number as a universal rule.

## Turn the evidence into a BUILD, CHANGE or KILL decision

Set decision criteria before collecting evidence.

- **BUILD:** The critical assumptions are sufficiently supported for the next investment. State what still needs testing during development.
- **CHANGE:** A real problem exists, but the evidence weakens the proposed target segment, value proposition, solution, buying process or commercial model. Change the weak element and test it again.
- **KILL:** Critical assumptions fail without a credible correction. Stop or pause before making a larger investment.

Killing a weak idea is a useful result when it prevents a larger loss. Changing a promising idea is also a validation outcome—not a failure to confirm the original plan.

## Common business idea validation mistakes

- Starting with the solution before understanding the problem.
- Interviewing friends, colleagues or other convenient people instead of realistic buyers or users.
- Combining users, buyers and decision-makers despite their different incentives.
- Asking leading questions that encourage agreement.
- Treating stated interest as a commercial commitment.
- Counting only positive responses and dismissing contradictions.
- Treating a fixed interview number as proof.
- Changing the decision criteria after seeing the evidence.

An independent interviewer can examine the same assumptions across participants, follow contradictory evidence and separate what was actually said from what the team hoped it meant.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is business idea validation?

Business idea validation is a structured process for testing the assumptions that must be true for an idea to become a viable product, service or business.

### How do you validate a business idea before building it?

Define the decision, identify the riskiest assumptions, select a realistic target group, gather existing market evidence, interview potential buyers or users, test the most important remaining assumptions and compare the evidence against decision criteria established in advance.

### How many customer interviews are needed?

There is no universal number. The appropriate scope depends on participant relevance, the number of customer segments, the commercial question and how much new information each interview adds. A focused project may use 20 interviews, while broader studies may require substantially more.

### Can customer interviews validate market demand?

Customer interviews can provide meaningful evidence about the strength, nature and conditions of demand. For some decisions, the next stage should add behavioural evidence such as a pilot, pre-sale, procurement step or purchase.

### What is the difference between market research and business idea validation?

Market research describes a market, its size, competitors and trends. Business idea validation tests the specific assumptions behind a particular idea and connects the evidence to a decision.

### When should an idea be changed instead of killed?

Change the idea when the evidence supports a meaningful customer problem but weakens an important part of the proposed solution, target segment, positioning, buying process or commercial model. Kill or pause it when critical assumptions fail and no credible adjustment resolves them.
